The rules

  1. Block out first. Get the proportions right with as few faces as possible, then add loops only where the shape needs them. A bad cage gives a bad result at every level.
  2. Quads only. Every face that goes into the subdivision has four sides. N-gons and triangles become poles with dents around them.
  3. Keep faces even. Neighbouring faces about the same size and roughly square. Long thin faces only where they are support loops.
  4. Support loops hold edges. The closer a loop sits to an edge, the sharper that edge. Bevel (Ctrl+B) with Shape 1 and 2 segments puts one loop on each side.
  5. Poles go on flat areas. A vertex with 3 or 5 edges is normal; put it where the surface is flat or hidden, never on a curve or a highlight. Avoid 6 or more.
  6. Loops follow the form. Run them along the curvature and around holes and bosses, not across them.
  7. Match segments. Parts that meet have the same number of edges where they meet. Cylinders in multiples of 4 (8, 12, 16, 24) so they fit a grid.
  8. Circles come from grids. 8 sides from a 2×2 block of faces, 16 from 4×4. Never cut a 32-sided hole into a single face.
  9. Apply the scale first. Ctrl+A → Scale before you bevel, or the bevels come out in different widths.
  10. Judge the result, not the cage. Model with the subdivision on and check it in a reflection matcap.
  11. Keep the modifier live. Hand in with the Subdivision Surface modifier still on the stack, not applied.

Support loops

The same 2 m cube, five ways. Add loops with Bevel (Ctrl+B, Shape 1, 2 segments), Loop Cut (Ctrl+R) or Edge → Offset Edge Slide (Ctrl+Shift+R).

Real objects are rarely razor sharp. Pick the loop distance from the real edge: a few millimetres on a phone, a centimetre on a table top. Keep that distance the same everywhere on one object.

Poles

Every mesh that isn't a flat grid has poles: a cube has eight 3-edge corners. They are fine where the surface is flat and calm. On a curve they leave a dent or a ripple you will see in every highlight.

Move a pole by sliding loops (G+G), by dissolving an edge (Ctrl+X) and cutting a new one with the Knife (K), or by rotating edges (Edge → Rotate Edge CW). Aim for flat areas, the underside, or behind another part.

N-gons and triangles

In this course every face going into the subdivision is a quad. Find the others with Select → Select All by Trait → Faces by Sides (Number of Vertices 4, Type Not Equal To). Turn triangle pairs into quads with Alt+J, and cut n-gons into quads with the Knife (K) or Connect Vertex Path (J).

Holes and bosses

How: select a 4×4 block of faces (for 8 sides: 2×2), Mesh → Transform → To Sphere (Shift+Alt+S), type 1. Inset (I) for the support loop, then delete the faces for a hole or Extrude (E) them for a boss. More segments need a bigger block: the rim has as many edges as the block's border.

Caps

The top of a 16-sided cylinder, three ways. All three have the same rim and support loops.

Grid Fill needs an even number of edges around the opening. 16 segments give a 4×4 middle, 12 give 3×3, 8 give 2×2.

Inside corners

An inward corner, like a notch in a seat, needs its own topology; no Bevel miter setting fixes it.

Apply the bevel, then add a diagonal from the corner (blue). The two support strips meet on it, and the rest of the face is cut into quads that continue to the outer edges. The vertex at the end of the diagonal has 5 edges: keep the face around it flat.
