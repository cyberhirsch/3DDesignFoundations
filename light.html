Colour temperature · Week 11

Colour temperature is measured in kelvin. It comes from an ideal glowing body: as it heats up, its light turns from red through white to light blue. Low is warm, high is cool.

The light that matches the white balance looks white. Raise the white balance and every light looks warmer: our morning grade uses 7,200 K. Lower it and the scene turns blue: the night uses 5,200 K.

Kelvin reference

Light sourceTemperatureColour

Street lamps and fluorescent tubes don’t glow like a hot body; their kelvin is the nearest match (the correlated colour temperature). A sodium lamp is far more orange and spiky than its 2,000 K swatch.

The swatches are display colours for a plain sRGB screen: the glow’s spectrum (Planck’s law) through the CIE 1931 colour matching functions, converted to sRGB, brightest channel set to 1, with 6,500 K shown as white, as in Unreal. They show the hue, not the brightness, and no screen can show the real thing.

The inverse square law · Week 11

Light from a small source spreads over a growing sphere. Twice the distance, the same light covers four times the area: a quarter of the light. A distant sun doesn’t fall off.

This is why a lamp far from its object needs absurd intensities, and why a directional sun lights near and far alike. It holds for small sources that shine in all directions; close to a large area light the light falls off more slowly. Unreal’s point and spot lights follow it while Use Inverse Squared Falloff is on, the default.

Real-world values

Your eyes adapt, so a room and a sunny street feel similar. The light meter disagrees by a factor of a thousand. Real values let you light a scene once and expose it like a camera.

EV100 here is what an incident light meter at ISO 100 would read: lux = 2.5 × 2EV100. Compare Epic’s rule of thumb from Week 12: a home 5 to 7, an office 7 to 8, a sunny day 15.

  • With real lux values the scene is bright or dark like a real place, so the exposure must be set like a camera: a 100,000 lux sun needs about EV100 15, a lamp-lit room about 5.
  • Blender’s units differ: point, spot and area lights in watts (radiant, not the bulb’s electrical watts), the sun in W/m². The manual suggests 1,000 W/m² for a clear sky, 0.001 for moonlight.

Exposure · Week 12

Exposure value (EV) counts how much light a camera lets in. One step, or stop, is twice the light. EV100 is the exposure value at ISO 100; Unreal’s exposure fields use it.

EV = log2(N² ÷ t)  ·  N = f-number, t = seconds  ·  EV100 = EV − log2(ISO ÷ 100)

  • Read the field names before you type. A project made in the Project Browser shows Min EV100 and Max EV100. Our example project was made another way and shows Min Brightness and Max Brightness, in cd/m²: there, 0 or below whites the frame out. The switch is Project Settings → Rendering → Default Settings → Extend default luminance range in Auto Exposure settings.
  • Auto exposure works like your eye: the bright window comes into frame and the room darkens. In a shot that looks like a mistake, so keep the exposure fixed through the whole shot.
  • In Unreal, a higher Min EV100 makes the picture darker: you expose for more light.

Three-point lighting · high key and low key

The key sets the main direction and form, the fill lifts the shadows, the rim draws an edge against the background. The key-to-fill ratio and the background decide whether the picture is high key or low key.

Start from
2 : 1
20%
0 stops

  • Key : fill here is key strength divided by fill strength. 2 : 1 is one stop between them, 4 : 1 two stops, 8 : 1 three.
  • High key: bright tones, soft light, low contrast, few shadows and none of them black. Low key: dark tones and large black areas; what is lit is where the eye goes.
  • One colour per light: red key, green fill, blue rim while you set up; where colours mix, two lights overlap. Set them back to white afterwards.
  • This head has no cast shadows and only diffuse light: it shows direction and ratio, not a final render.
